The fuller picture

Infections gave way to chronic disease. In 1900, four of the top ten causes were infections, and together they caused about a third of all deaths. In 2024, none of the top ten is an infection. Public health researchers call this shift the “epidemiologic transition.”

Most of the change came from how people lived. Clean water, sewers, safer food, better housing, and vaccines cut infectious deaths, many of them before antibiotics were widely available in the 1940s. Historians and economists still debate how much each one mattered.

Children gained the most. Diarrhea and diphtheria mostly killed infants and young children. Life expectancy at birth was so low in 1900 largely because so many people died young. Adults who reached old age then lived to ages we’d recognize.

Heart disease and cancer look worse than they are. Their rates rose because far more people now live to the ages when these diseases strike. Adjusted for age, death rates from heart disease have fallen sharply since the mid-1900s, and cancer death rates have been falling since the early 1990s.

Infections can come back. COVID-19 was the third leading cause of death in 2020 and 2021. By 2024 it had dropped out of the top ten, and life expectancy reached 79.0 years, the highest on record.

Not every cause is about germs or aging. Suicide and chronic liver disease are in today’s top ten, and drug overdoses account for many of the deaths counted as accidents. Sociologists study these because they follow social patterns such as region, work, and isolation.

Talk about it

  1. Which change between the two lists surprised you most?
  2. Clean water and sewers saved more lives than most medical treatments. Why do we tend to give doctors and medicines the credit?
  3. “Senility” was the ninth leading cause in 1900. Alzheimer’s disease is sixth today. What does it tell you that the names of causes change? Who decides what counts as a cause of death?
  4. Today’s leading causes are tied to age, diet, smoking, alcohol, and drugs. Are these personal choices, or are they shaped by where and how people live? What would a sociologist ask?
  5. These are national averages. Which groups do you think are doing better or worse than the average, and why?

About the data

1900: National Center for Health Statistics, “Leading Causes of Death, 1900–1998.” In 1900, only ten states and the District of Columbia kept death records good enough to count, so the figures describe those places, which were more urban than the country as a whole. Cause names are the ones used at the time; the 1900 label for stroke was “intracranial lesions of vascular origin.”

2024: National Center for Health Statistics, final mortality data. Rates here are deaths divided by population (crude rates), calculated from the published death counts and the overall rate of 903.4 per 100,000, so they can be compared with 1900. NCHS usually reports age-adjusted rates, which are different numbers.

Life expectancy: 47.3 years in 1900 and 79.0 in 2024, from NCHS life tables.

Sources: National Center for Health Statistics, “Leading Causes of Death, 1900–1998”; Xu, Jiaquan, Sherry L. Murphy, Kenneth D. Kochanek and Elizabeth Arias. 2026. “Mortality in the United States, 2024.” NCHS Data Brief No. 548; NCHS FastStats, “Deaths and Mortality.”
